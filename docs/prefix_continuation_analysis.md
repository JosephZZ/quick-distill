# Prefix Continuation Analysis: Teacher Follows Student's Reasoning Path

## Setup

We investigate how the teacher model (Qwen3-1.7B) behaves when continuing from a student-generated prefix, compared to generating independently from scratch. The experimental setup is as follows:

- **Student model:** Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B
- **Teacher model:** Qwen3-1.7B
- **Prefix length:** 100 tokens generated by the student
- **Three conditions compared:**
  1. **Student full response** -- Student generates the complete response (prefix + continuation).
  2. **Teacher continuation** -- Teacher continues from the student's 100-token prefix.
  3. **Teacher from scratch** -- Teacher generates the full response independently (no student prefix).

Eight problems from MATH-500 were examined across these three conditions.

## Key Finding

When the teacher continues from a student-generated prefix, it follows the student's reasoning path rather than its own independent path. The teacher's continuation is strongly conditioned on the content and style of the prefix, even when that prefix contains errors. This effect is most clearly demonstrated in Problem 8, where the student's prefix contains a mathematical error that the teacher adopts.

## Best Example: Problem 8 (Isosceles Right Triangle)

**Problem:** "In a certain isosceles right triangle, the altitude to the hypotenuse has length 4sqrt(2). What is the area of the triangle?"

### Student Prefix (100 tokens)

> "Let's start by understanding the problem. In an isosceles right triangle, the two legs are of equal length, and the hypotenuse is sqrt(2) times the length of a leg. If we denote the length of each leg by a, then the hypotenuse will be a*sqrt(2). The altitude to the hypotenuse in an isosceles right triangle is half the length of a leg, because the altitude bisects the"

Note the error in the prefix: it claims "altitude to hypotenuse = half the length of a leg." The correct formula is altitude = a / sqrt(2), or equivalently a * sqrt(2) / 2.

### Student Continuation

The student self-corrects in its continuation, using altitude = a * sqrt(2) / 2 = 4*sqrt(2), yielding a = 8 and area = 32 (correct).

### Teacher Continuation from Prefix

> "hypotenuse and splits the triangle into two congruent right triangles. ... Altitude = a/2 = 4*sqrt(2), so a = 8*sqrt(2), area = 1/2 * (8*sqrt(2))^2 = 64."

The teacher gets led astray by the prefix's claim about "half the length" and uses altitude = a/2. This produces the incorrect answer of 64.

### Teacher from Scratch

The teacher independently uses the correct formula h = ab/c = a^2 / (a*sqrt(2)) = a / sqrt(2). Setting a / sqrt(2) = 4*sqrt(2), it obtains a = 8 and area = 32 (correct).

### Summary

The same teacher model arrives at different answers depending on whether it continues from the student's prefix (incorrect: 64) or generates independently (correct: 32). The prefix's erroneous claim about the altitude formula directly causes the teacher to adopt the wrong reasoning.

## Implications for Distillation

### 1. Teacher scoring is conditioned on student context

When computing teacher log-probabilities for reverse KL loss, the teacher's distribution is conditioned on the student-generated prefix. This means the teacher and student distributions are more aligned than they would be if computed independently. The teacher "adapts" to the student's reasoning style and vocabulary choices, rather than imposing its own preferred solution path.

### 2. This explains why positional distillation works

Since the teacher follows the student's reasoning path after the prefix, the KL divergence between student and teacher is primarily about surface-level differences (formatting, LaTeX conventions, token-level word choices) rather than fundamental reasoning disagreements. Distilling on early tokens nudges the student toward better local choices at the start of the response, which then propagate through autoregressive generation. The teacher is not trying to redirect the student toward a completely different solution strategy; it is refining the student's existing approach.

### 3. Quantitative validation on MATH-500

We evaluated prefix continuation on the full MATH-500 benchmark (n_samples=4, temperature=0.7) across three prefix lengths: 100, 200, and 300 tokens. This matches the evaluation protocol used for all other distillation experiments (avg@4, maj@4, pass@4).

#### Baselines

| Configuration | avg@4 | maj@4 | pass@4 |
|---------------|:-----:|:-----:|:------:|
| Student baseline (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) | 50.95% | 61.20% | 72.80% |
| Teacher baseline (Qwen3-1.7B) | 65.30% | 68.40% | 77.00% |

#### Student prefix → Teacher continuation

| Prefix length | avg@4 | maj@4 | pass@4 |
|:-------------:|:-----:|:-----:|:------:|
| 100 | 62.70% | 65.80% | 74.20% |
| 200 | 56.30% | 58.80% | 67.80% |
| 300 | 51.75% | 53.40% | 58.80% |

#### Teacher prefix → Student continuation

| Prefix length | avg@4 | maj@4 | pass@4 |
|:-------------:|:-----:|:-----:|:------:|
| 100 | 47.00% | 55.60% | 67.80% |
| 200 | 47.45% | 52.00% | 65.00% |
| 300 | 52.65% | 57.80% | 65.80% |

#### Analysis

**Student prefix → Teacher continuation (teacher carries):**

- At prefix=100, the teacher achieves 62.70% avg@4, close to its own baseline of 65.30% -- only a 2.6-point drop. The teacher can largely recover from a short student prefix.
- At prefix=200, performance drops to 56.30%. The student's reasoning choices are becoming more entrenched and harder for the teacher to course-correct.
- At prefix=300, performance falls to 51.75%, essentially at the student baseline. By this point, the student has committed to a solution path (often with errors), and the teacher cannot recover.

This shows a clear degradation curve: the longer the student prefix, the more the teacher is "locked in" to the student's reasoning trajectory. The teacher's ability to improve upon the student's work diminishes rapidly with prefix length.

**Teacher prefix → Student continuation (student follows):**

- At prefix=100, the student achieves only 47.00% avg@4, actually *below* the student baseline of 50.95%. The teacher's short prefix uses a different style and vocabulary (markdown headings, `$$` math blocks) that appears to confuse the student rather than help it.
- At prefix=200, similar at 47.45%.
- At prefix=300, performance recovers to 52.65%, slightly above baseline. By this point the teacher has completed enough of the reasoning that the student only needs to finish the computation.

The poor performance with short teacher prefixes suggests a style mismatch penalty: the student is not trained on teacher-style text, and must "adapt" to an unfamiliar prefix before it can reason effectively. This is consistent with the observation that the two models use fundamentally different formatting conventions (`\( \)` vs `$`, numbered lists vs markdown headings).

**Key takeaway:** Performance is dominated by the continuation model, not the prefix model. The teacher's continuation ability (62.70% from student prefix at 100 tok) far exceeds the student's continuation ability (47.00% from teacher prefix at 100 tok). A good prefix cannot compensate for weak continuation, but a strong continuator can partially recover from a weak prefix -- at least when the prefix is short enough.

### 4. Risk of reinforcing student errors

If the student's prefix contains errors, the teacher may follow along rather than correct them, as demonstrated in Problem 8. This can produce misleading training signal: the teacher's continuation endorses the student's mistake, and the reverse KL loss would train the student to be more confident in the erroneous reasoning. This suggests that distillation is most effective on tokens where the student has not yet committed to a wrong path -- that is, the earliest tokens of the response, before substantive mathematical claims are made. This provides additional justification for the positional distillation approach, which focuses the loss on the first N response tokens.

The quantitative results confirm this directly: the teacher's performance degrades from 62.70% (prefix=100) to 51.75% (prefix=300) as it becomes increasingly constrained by the student's reasoning. The window for effective distillation is early in the response, before the student's errors become load-bearing.
