% ABSTRACT
% ===========================================================================
\begin{abstract}
On-policy knowledge distillation (KD) trains a student model on reverse KL loss over its own generated sequences.
The standard approach computes loss uniformly across all token positions, implicitly assuming each position contributes equally.
We show this assumption is wrong and harmful: teacher signal quality \emph{decays with position}, as later tokens increasingly reflect the teacher confirming the student's prefix rather than providing independent judgment.
We identify an \emph{information-quality paradox}: tokens with the highest KL divergence---seemingly the most informative---produce the worst distillation outcomes (53.4\% on MATH-500), while a contiguous prefix capturing only 46\% of total KL signal achieves 65.85\%.
High-KL tokens correspond to format and style disagreements (LaTeX notation, code blocks), not reasoning quality.
Based on this analysis, we propose \emph{positional distillation}: computing loss only on the first $N$ response tokens---a one-line code change (\texttt{loss\_mask[:, N:] = 0}).
Positional distillation achieves 12--24$\times$ wall-clock speedup, 4$\times$ memory reduction, and matches or exceeds full-sequence performance across 3 model families (Qwen, Gemma, Llama), 3 tasks (mathematical reasoning, function calling, code generation), and 2 training methods (LoRA, full fine-tuning).
Cross-model KL profiles predict optimal position limits, and a \emph{cascade effect} propagates early-token improvements to untrained positions, reducing KL by 35--38\% beyond the trained range.
On function calling, distilled students exceed teacher performance by up to 7.3 percentage points, suggesting the method extracts distributional reasoning signal while filtering surface-level noise.
\end{abstract}

% 1. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On-policy knowledge distillation trains a student on reverse KL loss over its own generated sequences, avoiding the train-test mismatch of off-policy methods \citep{agarwal2024onpolicy, gu2024minillm}.
This pipeline is increasingly adopted for distilling reasoning \citep{deepseekr1, chen2025distillingessence} and structured generation capabilities \citep{xu2024survey}.

Standard practice computes loss uniformly over all generated tokens, treating each position as equally informative.
We show this assumption is fundamentally flawed.
Full-sequence on-policy distillation is fragile: 2 of 3 Qwen seeds exhibit accuracy collapse, Gemma drops \emph{below baseline}, and Llama function calling degrades from 55.3\% to 32.0\%.
These failures are symptoms of a structural problem.

\textbf{Core insight: teacher signal quality decays with position.}
In on-policy distillation, the teacher scores token $t$ conditioned on the student's prefix $y_{<t}$.
At early positions, the teacher provides independent judgment---its distribution reflects genuine knowledge about how to approach a problem.
At later positions, the teacher is increasingly conditioned on the student's prior choices, reducing its role to confirming or rubber-stamping what the student already decided.
We quantify this decay across 59,936 trajectories: mean KL divergence falls from 1.91 at positions 0--50 to 0.43 at positions 150--200 (4.4$\times$ reduction), teacher entropy decreases from 0.26 to 0.17, and teacher-student agreement rises from 75.4\% to 88.8\%.

\textbf{The information-quality paradox.}
A natural response to signal decay would be to train on tokens with the highest KL divergence---targeting where teacher and student disagree most.
We show this intuition is precisely wrong.
Training on the top-100 highest-KL tokens captures 93\% of total KL signal but achieves only 53.4\% on MATH-500.
Training on the first 100 positional tokens captures merely 46\% of KL signal yet achieves 65.85\%.
The resolution: high-KL tokens are dominated by LaTeX formatting disagreements (mean KL = 3.99) and stylistic preferences, not reasoning quality.
The teacher's confusion about format is not useful supervision.

\textbf{Solution: positional distillation.}
We propose \emph{positional distillation}: computing loss only on the first $N$ response tokens and clamping generation accordingly.
This yields a 12$\times$ wall-clock speedup (by generating $N$ tokens instead of $T \gg N$) and 1.9$\times$ memory reduction, while matching or exceeding full-sequence performance across 3 model families, 3 tasks, and 2 training methods.
A \emph{cascade effect} propagates early-token improvements to untrained positions, reducing KL by 35--38\% beyond the trained range.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Signal quality analysis.} We show that teacher supervision quality in on-policy KD decays with position across three model families, driven by increasing conditioning on the student's prefix. Cross-model KL profiles predict optimal position limits.
  \item \textbf{Information-quality paradox.} We demonstrate that high-KL tokens correspond to format/style noise, not reasoning quality. Training on them produces the worst outcomes among seven token selection strategies, overturning the intuition that largest errors are most informative.
  \item \textbf{Cross-family validation.} Positional distillation matches or exceeds full-sequence performance across 3 families, 3 tasks, and 2 training methods, with 12--24$\times$ speedup. On function calling, students exceed teacher accuracy by up to 7.3pp.
  \item \textbf{Cascade effect.} We show that early-token distillation changes entire generation trajectories, with KL reduction extending 35--38\% to untrained positions---the method's benefits are not confined to the trained window.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Concurrent work.}
\citet{chen2025distillingessence} observe that prefix truncation is an efficient heuristic for reasoning distillation.
\citet{li2025preplan} analyze reward decay with depth in reinforcement learning.
Our work differs in three ways: (i)~we explain \emph{why} prefix truncation works through signal quality analysis and the information-quality paradox, (ii)~we show it is \emph{better}, not merely faster, than full-sequence training, and (iii)~we validate across 3 model families and 3 tasks including function calling where students exceed teacher performance.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{On-Policy Knowledge Distillation}

Knowledge distillation transfers knowledge from a teacher $q_\phi$ to a student $p_\theta$ \citep{hinton2015distilling}.
On-policy distillation \citep{agarwal2024onpolicy, gu2024minillm} generates sequences from the student's distribution, with the standard reverse KL objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\text{full} = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} D_\mathrm{KL}\!\left( q_\phi(\cdot | x, y_{<t}) \;\|\; p_\theta(\cdot | x, y_{<t}) \right) \right],
\label{eq:fullseq}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the prompt, $y \sim p_\theta$ is the student-generated response, and $T$ is the response length.
Crucially, the teacher distribution $q_\phi(\cdot|x, y_{<t})$ is conditioned on the student's prefix $y_{<t}$, which becomes increasingly constraining as $t$ grows.

\subsection{Token Selection in Training}

\citet{wang2025beyond8020} show that high-entropy tokens drive RL, finding the top 20\% contribute 80\% of learning signal.
\citet{huang2025selectkd} propose entropy-weighted KD losses; \citet{xie2026adakd} and \citet{kim2026tsdkd} introduce adaptive token-level distillation.
These methods select tokens by signal magnitude, assuming higher-signal tokens are more informative.
We show this assumption fails for on-policy KD: highest-KL tokens are format noise, and \emph{position} matters more than magnitude.

\subsection{Concurrent Work}

\citet{chen2025distillingessence} independently discover that sequence truncation improves distillation efficiency, but do not analyze why or compare against token selection baselines.
\citet{li2025preplan} show reward quality degrades with depth in RL---complementary to our signal decay finding, though they do not propose position-restricted loss.
Our work provides the explanatory framework (signal decay, information-quality paradox), predictive tools (KL profiles), and cross-family validation these concurrent works lack.

\section{Teacher Signal Quality Decays with Position}
\label{sec:analysis}

We present the analytical findings that motivate positional distillation.
All analysis uses Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (student) and Qwen3-1.7B (teacher) with 59,936 on-policy trajectories (mean length 386 tokens) unless noted otherwise.

\subsection{The Conditional Scoring Problem}
\label{sec:conditional}

In on-policy distillation, the teacher scores token $t$ via $q_\phi(\cdot | x, y_{<t}^{\text{student}})$---conditioned on the student's prefix.
At early positions, the short prefix leaves the teacher freedom to express independent preferences over strategies, creating genuine supervision.
At late positions, the long prefix constrains the teacher into confirming the student's trajectory---the signal degrades from independent judgment to passive confirmation.
This asymmetry is structural: any autoregressive model conditioned on a long prefix will have its distribution narrowed, regardless of quality.

The highest-KL tokens are LaTeX formatting delimiters (\texttt{\textbackslash(} with KL = 10.3, \texttt{\textbackslash[} with KL = 13.2 across 28K occurrences).
These reflect different conventions for inline vs.\ display math between the Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen3 model families---a training data artifact, not reasoning quality.
Planning tokens (``To'', ``First'', ``Therefore'') have moderate KL and genuinely reflect strategic disagreements.
Math computation tokens (numbers, operators) have near-zero KL: teacher and student agree on arithmetic.

The composition shifts dramatically with position: positions 0--4 contain 33\% planning tokens and 2\% math numbers, while positions 200--500 contain 8\% planning and 18\% math numbers.
Early positions are \emph{strategic} (what approach to take); later positions are \emph{mechanical} (executing computations where both models agree).
This explains the KL decay: early positions encode decisions where the teacher has independent judgment; later positions encode execution where agreement is high.

The result is striking: Top-KL-100 captures 93\% of total KL signal but achieves only 53.4\%---7.3pp \emph{worse} than Prefix-100, which captures only 46\%.
More signal concentration yields worse outcomes.
Middle-100 (47.8\%) and Last-100 (50.4\%) perform \emph{below the undistilled baseline} (51.0\%), demonstrating that late-position tokens are not merely uninformative but actively harmful.

\textbf{Resolution.}
The paradox resolves through the token classification (Section~\ref{sec:token_class}).
High-KL tokens are dominated by LaTeX formatting disagreements where the teacher is \emph{confused by} the student's formatting choices, not \emph{superior to} the student's reasoning.
Direct measurement confirms this: top-KL tokens have teacher entropy of 0.449 nats---nearly double the 0.235 of prefix tokens---indicating the teacher is \emph{uncertain}, not \emph{superior}, at these positions.
Training on these tokens teaches superficial format preferences, not mathematical reasoning.

The prefix succeeds for a different reason: the \emph{cascade effect}.
In autoregressive generation, each token conditions all subsequent tokens.
Improving early decisions---solution strategy, problem decomposition---rewrites the entire generation trajectory.
Scattered high-KL tokens, by contrast, are isolated corrections that do not propagate.

\section{Method: Positional Distillation}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Formulation}

Positional distillation restricts the loss in Eq.~\ref{eq:fullseq} to the first $N$ response tokens:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pos-}N} = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{N} D_\mathrm{KL}\!\left( q_\phi(\cdot | x, y_{<t}) \;\|\; p_\theta(\cdot | x, y_{<t}) \right) \right].
\label{eq:pos}
\end{equation}

The implementation is a single line: \texttt{loss\_mask[:, N:] = 0}.
Generation length is clamped to $N$ (\texttt{max\_new\_tokens = N}), so the student generates exactly $N$ tokens per problem.
The teacher scores these $N$ tokens with a single forward pass.
No changes to the model architecture, optimizer, or training loop are required.

\subsection{Selecting $N$}
\label{sec:selecting_n}

We recommend the following heuristic: generate $\sim$1000 on-policy trajectories, compute per-position KL, and select $N$ at approximately 40--50\% of cumulative KL coverage.
For front-loaded profiles (Qwen-like), this yields $N = 50$--100.
For flat profiles (Llama-like), $N = 150$--200.
In practice, performance is robust to the exact choice: for Qwen, $N \in [50, 200]$ all achieve within 1pp of the optimum (Table~\ref{tab:position_sweep}).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Model families.}
We evaluate three student-teacher pairs spanning different architectures and capability gaps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Qwen}: Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B $\to$ Qwen3-1.7B (cross-architecture, math-specialized student)
  \item \textbf{Gemma}: Gemma-2-2B-IT $\to$ Gemma-3-4B-IT (cross-generation, instruction-tuned)
  \item \textbf{Llama}: Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct $\to$ Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (cross-generation, 8$\times$ size gap)
\end{itemize}
Additionally, we test Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B $\to$ Qwen3-4B (larger teacher) and Qwen3-4B $\to$ Qwen3-8B (same-family negative result).

\textbf{Tasks.}
(i)~\textbf{MATH-500} \citep{hendrycks2021measuring}: mathematical reasoning, evaluated with avg@4 (4 samples, temperature 0.7).
(ii)~\textbf{BFCL}: function calling (600 problems), evaluated with full accuracy (function name + arguments).
(iii)~\textbf{HumanEval/MBPP} \citep{chen2021evaluating, austin2021program}: code generation, evaluated with pass@1 (temperature 0.0).

\textbf{Training.}
LoRA \citep{hu2022lora}: $r=32$, $\alpha=64$, all linear layers, $\text{lr}=5\times10^{-5}$.
Full fine-tuning: $\text{lr}=5\times10^{-6}$.
All: 200 steps, 3200 training problems, $n_{\text{samples}}=1$, batch size 16, cosine LR schedule, checkpoints every 50 steps.
We report best checkpoint across steps 50--200.

\subsection{Degradation Analysis}
\label{sec:degradation}

Full-sequence distillation exhibits three failure modes:
(1)~\textbf{Repetition collapse (Qwen)}: by step 100, 91.5\% of responses repeat \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} an average of 88 times, inflating response length 3.3$\times$ and corrupting answer extraction. A relaxed metric (ground truth anywhere in response) shows only 4.3pp true degradation vs.\ the apparent 19.3pp.
(2)~\textbf{Below-baseline collapse (Gemma)}: full-sequence achieves 11.70\% LoRA / 13.90\% FullFT, both below the 13.45\% baseline.
(3)~\textbf{Capability destruction (Llama funcall)}: function calling drops from 55.3\% to 32.0\% despite 99\%+ parse rate---full-sequence training on 2048 tokens overwhelms the 33-token function call structure.


% ===========================================================================
% 6. DISCUSSION
% ===========================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Positional Distillation Works}

Three mechanisms explain the effectiveness:
(1)~\textbf{Signal quality filtering}: restricting to early positions avoids late tokens where the teacher is confirmatory (agreement 89\%, entropy 0.17) rather than informative (agreement 75\%, entropy 0.26 at early positions). Full-sequence training dilutes high-quality signal with low-quality confirmation.
(2)~\textbf{Noise avoidance}: late-position KL is dominated by format disagreements that teach style preferences rather than reasoning, creating high-variance gradients that cause repetition collapse and below-baseline degradation.
(3)~\textbf{Cascade effect}: improving early decisions (strategy, structure) rewrites entire generation trajectories. The 35--38\% KL reduction at untrained positions is direct evidence of this amplification.

\subsection{Why Students Exceed Teachers}

On function calling, distilled students exceed teacher accuracy by up to 7.3pp.
This occurs because on-policy distillation decouples the teacher's \emph{distributional knowledge} from its \emph{generation ability}.
The Qwen3-1.7B teacher cannot generate valid JSON (it outputs natural language), yet its distribution assigns non-trivial mass to correct function names and arguments.
The student, training on-policy against this distribution, learns to produce the structured output the teacher ``knows'' distributionally.
Positional distillation amplifies this by focusing on positions where function call structure resides ($<$100 tokens), avoiding positions where the teacher reverts to natural language.
This implies that a model's distributional knowledge can substantially exceed its generation-time performance.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Task scope.}
Our validation covers structured reasoning tasks (math, function calling, code); benefits for creative or open-ended generation are unclear.
\textbf{Model scale.}
All experiments use models $\le$4B (students) and $\le$8B (teachers); whether signal decay persists at larger scales is an open question.
\textbf{Loss type.}
We evaluate reverse KL only; other divergences may exhibit different positional structure.
\textbf{$N$ selection.}
The 40--50\% cumulative KL heuristic is descriptive; a principled theoretical account is future work.


% ===========================================================================
% 7. CONCLUSION
% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

We identify a fundamental positional structure in on-policy knowledge distillation: teacher signal quality decays with position as supervision transitions from independent judgment to passive confirmation.
This resolves an information-quality paradox---high-KL tokens are format noise, not reasoning signal---and motivates positional distillation: \texttt{loss\_mask[:, N:] = 0}.
Across 3 model families, 3 tasks, and 2 training methods, positional distillation matches or exceeds full-sequence performance with 12--24$\times$ speedup, eliminates collapse and instability, and enables students to exceed teachers by up to 7.3pp on function calling.
In on-policy distillation, \emph{where} the teacher teaches matters more than \emph{how much} it teaches.

\section{Token Classification Details}
\label{app:token_class}

Token classification was performed on 2,015,036 tokens from 10,000 trajectories using rule-based categorization over the Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B tokenizer vocabulary.

\textbf{Categories:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{planning}: Sentence-initial strategy words (``To'', ``Let'', ``First'', ``Therefore'', ``Thus'', ``Since'', ``Given'', etc.)
\item \textbf{structural}: Formatting tokens (\texttt{**}, \texttt{:}, newlines, \texttt{\$}, parentheses, brackets)
\item \textbf{math\_latex}: LaTeX-specific commands (\texttt{\textbackslash(}, \texttt{\textbackslash[}, \texttt{\textbackslash)}, \texttt{\textbackslash]}, \texttt{\textbackslash frac}, etc.)
\item \textbf{math\_number}: Digit tokens (0--9)
\item \textbf{math\_operator}: Arithmetic operators ($=$, $+$, $-$, $\times$, $/$ etc.)
\item \textbf{continuation}: All remaining tokens (common words, word fragments, etc.)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Position 0 deep dive.}
